# Microchimerism

> Persistent presence of a small number of cells from another individual in a host, typically at ratios of 1:10⁵–10⁶ host cells. Conceptually distinct from [[40-Topics/somatic-mosaicism]] (one zygote, multiple genotypes) and from full chimerism (one organism, multiple zygotes).

## Definition

Most common sources (synthesis):

- **Maternal microchimerism (MMC)** — mother's cells persist in offspring after transplacental transfer.
- **Fetal microchimerism (FMC)** — fetal cells persist in maternal tissues after pregnancy. 15% to >50% of mothers carry fetal cells in their blood that may persist for decades ([[10-Summaries/forsberg-2017-mosaicism-review]]).
- **Twin–twin transfusion** — exchange of cells between dizygotic twins sharing a placenta.
- **Iatrogenic** — transplant, transfusion.

## Why it matters

- Confounds clinical genetic testing — a "mosaic" variant detected in maternal blood may actually be of fetal origin (or vice versa).
- Has biological roles: tissue regeneration, wound healing, modulation of T-cell repertoires.
- Associated with autoimmune conditions (scleroderma, lupus) and tumor stromal biology.

## Variants and refinements

- Quantitative range: 1:100 (rare, high microchimerism) to 1:10⁶ (typical).
- Detection requires either targeted PCR for sex-mismatched markers (Y-chromosome assays after pregnancy with a male fetus) or HLA-mismatch sequencing.

## Contested points

- Clinical significance — whether the persistent presence of "foreign" cells contributes mechanistically to disease or is incidental remains debated for most conditions.

## Examples

- Y-chromosome cells in maternal blood decades after pregnancy with a male fetus.
- Donor cells from blood transfusions persisting in recipient circulation.
