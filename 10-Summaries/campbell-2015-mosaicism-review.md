# Campbell et al. 2015 — Somatic mosaicism: implications for disease and transmission genetics

> Thesis: the developmental timing of a post-zygotic mutation determines its distribution across body tissues — early mutations affect large fractions of the soma, mid-development mutations are confined to one side or to specific lineages, and late mutations are localized. This timing geometry has direct clinical implications for diagnostic detection, recurrence risk, and transmission to offspring.

## Key claims

- **Mosaicism vs chimerism**: mosaicism = multiple genotypes from one zygote; chimerism = multiple genotypes from multiple fertilization events.
- **Timing geometry of mutation distribution** (Fig 1):
  - First mitosis → ~50% of cells affected (e.g., CHILD syndrome with midline-demarcated phenotype).
  - Before left-right determination → both sides, possibly both gonads.
  - After left-right determination → confined to one side (one gonad at most).
  - After primordial germ cell (PGC) differentiation → soma-only or gonad-only, never both (gonosomal exclusion).
- **PGCs migrate out by day 24 post-fertilization**, after ~15 mitotic divisions. Mutations before this can be present in both somatic and germ tissues.
- **Up to 70% of human embryos show CNV or aneuploidy** in at least one blastomere during the first week — early development is unusually genome-unstable.
- **Placental mosaicism is found in 1–2% of chorionic villus samplings**, possibly reflecting either high early-embryonic mutation rates or selective segregation of mutant cells away from the embryo proper.
- **Major mosaic classes**: aneuploidy and structural rearrangements (trisomy 8, ring chromosomes, isochromosomes such as Pallister-Killian's i(12p)), CNVs, SNVs.
- **Constitutional vs mosaic phenotypes** of the same mutation can be very different — some mutations are only viable in the mosaic state (e.g., i(12p)).
- **Transmission/recurrence risk** depends on whether the mutation predates PGC differentiation.

## Methods / evidence

Synthesizing review with clinical case examples (CHILD syndrome, Proteus syndrome, hemimegalencephaly, linear and whorled nevoid hypermelanosis, Pallister-Killian). Authors from the Lupski lab at Baylor — major clinical genetics group.

## Surprising or load-bearing bits

- **The developmental timing → tissue distribution mapping** is the load-bearing observation: a mutation's *when* almost entirely determines its *where*. This is the basis for inferring the timing of a mutation from a phenotype's distribution, and for predicting transmission risk.
- **Up to 70% of embryos with at least one aneuploid blastomere** at week 1 — but most adult tissues are euploid. Selection against aneuploid cells during embryogenesis is intense.
- **PGC bottleneck and timing** is the key clinical consideration for recurrence risk: if a mutation arose before PGC differentiation, it may be transmitted to multiple offspring; if after, it cannot reach the germline.

## Open questions

- The exact timing of left-right determination in humans — Ian 2015 notes it's known to be ~8-cell in mice but unknown in humans.
- Whether the elevated mutability of early-development cells is intrinsic (e.g., maternal-cytoplasm replication machinery) or reflects relaxed selection — still open in 2025.
- Quantitative recurrence-risk estimates as a function of estimated mutation timing — could be substantially improved by single-cell methods.
