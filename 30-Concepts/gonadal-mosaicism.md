# Gonadal mosaicism

> Subclass of [[somatic-mosaicism]] in which the mosaic mutation is present in the gonadal lineage (sperm or eggs) and therefore can be transmitted to multiple offspring even though the parent appears clinically unaffected. A major source of unexplained recurrence in apparently de novo dominant genetic disorders.

## Definition

A post-zygotic mutation that arises before primordial germ cell (PGC) differentiation (within the first ~15 mitotic divisions, by day 24 post-fertilization) can populate the gonad with mutated germ cells alongside (or instead of) somatic cells ([[10-Summaries/campbell-2015-mosaicism-review]]).

- **Gonadal-only mosaicism** — mutation confined to germline; parent has normal somatic phenotype but transmits the mutation.
- **Gonosomal mosaicism** — present in both soma and germline; parent may have mild somatic phenotype + transmits to offspring.

## Why it matters

- **Recurrence risk in apparently sporadic disorders**: an unaffected parent of an affected child may carry gonadal mosaicism. A second affected child is then not a coincidence but a transmission event.
- **Standard genetic counseling underestimates risk** unless gonadal mosaicism is considered for sibling-recurrence cases of apparently de novo dominant disorders.
- **Detection requires germ cell sampling** — invasive (testis/ovary biopsy) and rarely performed; bulk somatic DNA can miss it entirely.

## Variants and refinements

- **Male gonadal mosaicism** — detectable by sperm DNA analysis (non-invasive).
- **Female gonadal mosaicism** — requires invasive ovarian biopsy; rarely confirmed in life.

## Contested points

- Empirical recurrence risk estimates for specific disorders are uncertain — large family-history datasets are needed to quantify.

## Examples

- Apparently sporadic Duchenne muscular dystrophy families with multiple affected siblings — gonadal mosaicism is now recognized as a major mechanism.
