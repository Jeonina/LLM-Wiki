# Microchimerism

> Persistent presence of a small number of cells from another individual in a host, typically at ratios of 1:10⁵–10⁶ host cells. Conceptually distinct from [[40-Topics/somatic-mosaicism]] (one zygote, multiple genotypes) and from full chimerism (one organism, multiple zygotes) ([[10-Summaries/forsberg-2017-mosaicism-review|Forsberg 2017]]).

> [!abstract] 한눈에
> Microchimerism은 다른 개체에서 온 소수의 세포가 숙주 안에 오래 남아 있는 상태로, 한 zygote에서 유래한 somatic mosaicism과는 다른 현상이다 ([[10-Summaries/forsberg-2017-mosaicism-review|Forsberg 2017]]). 어머니의 15%에서 50% 이상이 혈액에 태아 세포를 수십 년간 지닐 수 있어 드물지 않다 ([[10-Summaries/forsberg-2017-mosaicism-review|Forsberg 2017]]).

## Definition

Microchimerism means cells from another individual are present in the soma, which makes it a distinct phenomenon from post-zygotic variation ([[10-Summaries/forsberg-2017-mosaicism-review|Forsberg 2017]]). The quantitative range runs from 1:100 (rare, high microchimerism) to 1:10⁶ (typical).

## Why it matters

- **It confounds clinical genetic testing** — a "mosaic" variant detected in maternal blood may actually be of fetal origin (or vice versa).
- **It has biological roles** — tissue regeneration, wound healing, modulation of T-cell repertoires.
- **It has disease associations** — autoimmune conditions (scleroderma, lupus) and tumour stromal biology.

## Sources of microchimeric cells

Most common sources (synthesis):

- **Maternal microchimerism (MMC)** — mother's cells persist in offspring after transplacental transfer.
- **Fetal microchimerism (FMC)** — fetal cells persist in maternal tissues after pregnancy.
- **Twin–twin transfusion** — exchange of cells between dizygotic twins sharing a placenta.
- **Iatrogenic** — transplant, transfusion.

## Detection

- Detection requires either targeted PCR for sex-mismatched markers (Y-chromosome assays after pregnancy with a male fetus) or HLA-mismatch sequencing.

## Evidence and examples

- 15% to >50% of mothers carry fetal cells in their blood that may persist for decades ([[10-Summaries/forsberg-2017-mosaicism-review|Forsberg 2017]]).
- Y-chromosome cells in maternal blood decades after pregnancy with a male fetus.
- Donor cells from blood transfusions persisting in recipient circulation.

## Contested points

- **Clinical significance** — whether the persistent presence of "foreign" cells contributes mechanistically to disease or is incidental remains debated for most conditions.
