**Citation:** Coorens et al. (2021) — *Extensive phylogenies of human development inferred from somatic mutations* — *Nature*. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-021-03790-y)

Coorens, Moore and colleagues (Sanger Institute / Stratton lab) reconstructed cellular phylogenies of normal human tissues from three adult individuals using 511 laser-capture microdissections subjected to low-input WGS. By treating shared somatic mutations as naturally occurring lineage barcodes, they built phylogenetic trees that resolve embryonic patterning at single-cell ancestral resolution.

Three principal findings. (1) The two reconstructed daughter cells of the zygote contribute asymmetrically to the adult body, with daughter-cell ratios ranging from 60:40 to 93:7 across individuals — asymmetries that persist across subsequent generations. (2) Mutation rates are high in the first cell division (~2.4 per cell per generation) and drop sharply afterward (~0.7 per cell per generation), consistent with later activation of the zygotic genome and DNA-repair machinery. (3) Phylogenies reveal a comb-like topology — most genetic sharing occurs in a short time window at the top of the tree — and resolve contiguous patches of ~301 colonic crypts derived from a single embryonic cell, plus spatially structured asymmetries in brain development. Data from 10 additional men suggest an extraembryonic contribution to primordial germ cells.

## Why this matters

Foundational demonstration that bulk-genotyped microdissection-WGS, rather than single-cell sequencing, can reconstruct human developmental phylogenies at scale. Establishes the asymmetric-zygote bottleneck, the early-then-low mutation-rate profile, and the spatial heterogeneity of embryonic contribution. Anchors §1 (lineage-tracing motivation), §5 (development applications), and complements scWGS-based single-neuron phylogenies (Lodato 2015, Bae 2018) by spanning entire bodies rather than single tissues.
